# Competitor research

A competitor is anyone the practice's buyer could hire instead of the
practice, including nobody. The buyer is whoever the brief names. When the
brief does not name one, the profile in `prospect-research` applies:
owner-operated specialty retail in the practice's region.

`research-sourcing` governs every fact below. Nothing here is cited from
memory, and the figures in this file are examples dated 2026-09-11 that are
re-retrieved before they reach a deliverable.

## When to activate

- A brief asks who the practice competes with, and on what.
- A brief asks what independent consultants or fractional engineering leaders
  charge, how they structure engagements, or where they find clients.
- `quarterly-planning` needs a market layer and none exists in the wiki.
- The owner is choosing a rate, a pricing model, or a positioning statement and
  wants the field mapped first.

Do not activate to decide the rate or the positioning. Those are the owner's. This
skill maps the field and names the options.

## Step 1: define the competitive set

Write the set down before searching. Four rings, and the brief says which
rings are in scope.

| Ring | Definition | Example for the practice |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Same buyer, same offer | An independent engineer selling web and systems work to small retailers in the same counties |
| Adjacent, same buyer | Same buyer, different offer | A local marketing agency that also builds sites for retailers |
| Adjacent, same offer | Same offer, different buyer | A fractional CTO serving venture-funded startups |
| Substitute | Neither, but takes the budget | A marketplace, a platform's own site builder, a nephew, doing nothing. **Correction 2026-09-11:** this row named Toptal, A.Team and Upwork. A.Team's own site states a Fortune 500 buyer and names HCA, Northern Trust, Lululemon and Blackstone, so it is not reachable by a small business the way Toptal's self-serve intake is. Upwork returned a retrieval failure and was not re-checked. Test a marketplace's stated buyer before placing it in this ring |

The substitute ring is the one most research skips and the one the buyer
weighs first. "Doing nothing" is a competitor with a price of zero and a cost
the dossier has to make visible.

## Step 2: find them

Search patterns, in order of yield as found on 2026-09-11:

1. Titles. "fractional CTO" plus a geography; "independent software
   consultant" plus a vertical; "fractional head of engineering".
2. Marketplaces and communities, as leads only: fractional job boards, Toptal,
   A.Team, Umbrex, LinkedIn profiles with "fractional" in the title.
3. The buyer's side. Who built the sites of the practice's prospects. A footer
   credit, a page source comment, a portfolio page. This is the direct ring
   and it is invisible from the seller's side.
4. Local: the county's business associations, chamber directories, and
   co-working member lists, as tier 3 leads.

Every lead goes to the competitor's own site or profile before it is
profiled. A directory listing is not a profile.

## Step 3: profile each one

The same fields for every competitor, so the comparison table has no holes
that are really omissions. A field with no public answer is recorded as "no
public figure", which is a finding.

| Field | What to record | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Legal name, trading name, location, entity status if a state registry covers it | 1 |
| Positioning | Their statement, verbatim. Then in Stark's X and Y form: who they help, with what outcome | 1 for the quote |
| Offer shape | Advisory, hands-on delivery, or both. Retainer, project, hourly, productized, subscription | 1 |
| Published rates | The figure and the page, dated. "Contact us" is a finding | 1 |
| Engagement shape | Hours or days per week, minimum term, number of concurrent clients if stated | 1 or 2 |
| Proof | Named clients, case studies with outcomes, testimonials. Note which are verifiable | 2 |
| Channels | Where they publish, how often, dated last post. Newsletter, talks, podcast, community | 2 |
| Credentials | Prior roles, certifications, years, as they state them | 1 for the claim |
| Buyer | Who they say they serve. Vertical, size, stage, geography | 1 |
| Signals | Hiring, new service pages, price changes, since the last run | 2 |

Positioning is recorded in the competitor's words first and translated second.
The translation is the analyst's reading and is labelled as one.

### Website structure

Added 2026-09-11. The table above records what a competitor says. It records
nothing about how the site is built to say it, and a brief that asks what a
competitor website contains was assigned to this skill with no fields to fill.
Collect these alongside the ten above whenever the brief asks about websites.

| Field | What to record | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Hero claim | The first claim above the fold, verbatim. A site with no hero records "not present", confirmed by a second retrieval | 1 for the quote |
| Page inventory | Every destination in the primary navigation and in the footer, listed | 1 |
| Home section order | Top to bottom, one line describing each section | 1 |
| Proof types present | Counted, not just noted: case studies, named client logos, testimonials, stated metrics, published writing | 1 |
| Primary call to action | Its label verbatim, its placement, and how many times it repeats | 1 |
| Price disclosure | Own rate, market rates quoted about others, price framing with no number, or none. These are four different answers | 1 |

Four rules that cost a run to learn.

**Retrieve the home page as well as the deep link.** A brief that names a
service page still needs the home page, because navigation, section order and
call-to-action placement are properties of the home page. On 2026-09-11 doing
this doubled the page count on one slice and changed four of five buyer fields.

**"A rate appears" has four answers, not two.** A site that publishes its own
price, a site that quotes the market's rates while publishing none of its own, a
site that frames price without a number, and a site silent on price are four
findings. Collapsing them into yes and no loses the most common case. On
2026-09-11 one slice of five had zero own rates, two quoting market figures, and
four framing price.

**Count proof where it is, and say where.** Proof on a service page and proof on
the home page are different facts, and one run found a competitor whose two
pages disagreed on its own client and testimonial counts. Record the page beside
the count.

**Say which page speaks for the site.** A firm whose home page sells one thing
and whose navigation sells another is one finding, not a contradiction to
resolve. Record both and let the brief's reader decide whether it is a
competitor for the buried offer.

## Step 4: the market layer

The comparison needs a field to sit in. Collect the market layer once per run
and cite it once.

### Pricing models

The taxonomy the sources use, with who advocates each and the failure modes
they name. Every entry below is what a named source says, not what the
practice has decided.

| Model | Mechanics | Advocates and critics, as of 2026-09-11 | Stated failure modes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Rate times hours, billed in arrears | Consulting Success reports roughly a quarter to a third of consultants use it and recommends new consultants start there. Stark ("Hourly Billing Is Nuts") and Weiss reject it | Rewards inefficiency; caps income at hours; puts consultant and client on opposite sides (Stark) |
| Day or week rate | Hourly times a multiplier; weekly is a marketplace convention (Toptal) | Liberman describes 8x to 10x hourly for a day | Same as hourly, coarser |
| Fixed price per project | One fee for one scope | Consulting Success reports it as the most common model in its survey. Umbrex splits it into time-and-materials advance agreement and value-based advance | Scope creep with no change process; underpricing when scope is vague |
| Access retainer | A period of access with a response-time guarantee, not a block of hours | Stark, Weiss, Liberman all describe it. Stark says it "is not prepayment for a block of hours" | Clients who cannot define the outcome they want (Stark); unrelated requests absorbed without stated objectives (Weiss) |
| Work retainer | A repeating set of deliverables per month | Liberman treats it as legitimate; Stark does not call it a retainer | Becomes an hourly contract with a different name |
| Fractional | Days or hours per week for a term, priced monthly | Go Fractional, Kompella, and the Fractional Work Report describe it. Stark places it between hands-on and advisory retainers | Capacity conflicts across clients; scope drift into full-time expectations |
| Value-based | Fee set from the outcome's value after conceptual agreement on objectives, measures, and value | Weiss, Enns. FIRMSconsulting argues it only works when the consultant is uniquely able to deliver the value. Thomson lists seven failure modes | Pricing before value is established; transactional buyers; thin lead flow forcing desperation pricing |
| Productized or subscription | A fixed offer at a fixed price, sold repeatedly | Stark treats the advisory retainer as one kind of productized subscription | Startup cost of defining the product; sales cycle differs from bespoke work |

Sources for the table: Consulting Success, "How to Set Consulting Fees",
updated 2026-09-07; Jonathan Stark, "Hourly Billing Is Nuts", "What exactly is
a monthly retainer?" (2025-01-25), "Everything You Ever Wanted To Know About
Retainers" (2021-06-28); Alan Weiss, "Consulting Tips from The Million Dollar
Consultant"; Melisa Liberman, "How Independent Consultant Rates and Retainer
Fees Work" (2024-03-11); Umbrex, "Price Your Services" (2026-03-30);
FIRMSconsulting, "Why Value-Based Consulting is Seldom a Good Idea" (updated
2023-06-07); Peter Thomson, "Why Value Pricing Fails" (2026-07-30); The Drum on
Blair Enns (2023-05-12). All (c) except the Consulting Success survey figures,
which are (a) with the caveat that n is stated and method is not. All
retrieved 2026-09-11 and re-retrieved before use.

### Rate-setting methods

Three methods appear in the sources, and the first one gives answers that
differ by about two times depending on the billable-hours assumption.

| Method | Formula as published | Assumption that moves the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Cost-plus or target income | Target income, grossed up for tax and expenses, divided by billable hours, plus a profit buffer (Toggl Track calculator) | Billable hours per year: Consulting Success assumes 960 (20 hours times 48 weeks); Toggl assumes 1,152 to 1,200 (60% of 2,000); Liberman's page states 1,960, with arithmetic that does not reconcile on the page |
| Market comparison | Position against published rates for the same role and buyer | Which survey. See the source register below |
| Value-based | Fee from the value of the outcome, agreed before the proposal (Weiss) | Whether the buyer accepts the value framing |

No solo-practitioner utilization benchmark with a data basis was found on
2026-09-11. The firm-level figure is SPI Research's 66.4% billable utilization
across 500+ firms for 2025, reported by Deltek on 2026-07-30. Toggl's 60% is
stated as "a realistic starting benchmark" with no data behind it. A
deliverable that uses any of these says which one and why.

### Where clients come from

Three surveys agree that referrals and prior employers dominate, and disagree
on the split. Consulting Success (2,800+): 24% of first clients from the most
recent employer, 28% from any previous employer, 21% cold outreach, 14%
referrals, 11% inbound. Fractional Work Report 2026 (1,733): 94% won clients
through network referrals. MBO 2025, as re-retrieved 2026-09-11: 42% of
independents used an online marketplace. Cite the survey and its n. Do not
blend them.

## Step 5: synthesize

Two artifacts, in this order.

**The comparison table.** One row per competitor, the profile fields as
columns, "no public figure" where that is the answer. Sort by ring, then by
proximity to the practice's buyer.

**The reading.** Labelled as the analyst's reading:

- Where the practice's offer sits on the model taxonomy, and who else sits
  there.
- What is claimed by everyone, which is therefore not a differentiator.
- What is claimed by nobody, which is either an opening or a reason.
- Where published rates cluster, with the caveats attached.
- Which competitors are visible to the buyer, judged from the buyer's side:
  local search, the prospects' own vendors, the chamber list.

Positioning tests to apply, as published, not as verdicts: Baker's five
pre-tests (a niche of roughly 2,000 to 10,000 prospects, twenty insights on
demand, a purchasable list, a constrained employee universe, someone will
travel for it; Punctuation, 2017-07-13, modified 2023-10-30) and Stark's X and
Y statement (who, and what business outcome; jonathanstark.com/xyps).

## Output

The deliverable is a `comparison`, in the `research-operations` format. Its
`Findings` section carries, in order: the competitive set and how it was
drawn, the comparison table, the market layer, the reading. `Decisions for
the owner` names the options the reading opens: at minimum, the pricing model
variants that fit the practice's capacity and the positioning statements the
field leaves open. `Gaps` carries every "no public figure" and every failed
retrieval.

## Biases

1. Published rates are marketing. A rate on a page is what the competitor
   wants to be seen charging.
2. A polished site is not a full pipeline.
3. Marketplace indexes are built from the marketplace's own listings.
4. A survey's n is not its method.
5. Everyone in the direct ring looks alike from the seller's side. The
   differences are visible from the buyer's side, so look from there.
